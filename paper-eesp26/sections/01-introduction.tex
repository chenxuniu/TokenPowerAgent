\section{Introduction}

LLM inference is a continuous operational workload and increasingly dominates
the lifecycle energy of deployed AI services.  Its energy cost is not fixed by
model size alone: workload profiles---including token lengths, concurrency,
and arrival rate---and serving configurations---including batching, KV-cache
controls, precision, inference engine, power cap, and tensor/pipeline
parallelism (TP/PP)---can materially change energy per token, throughput, time
to first token (TTFT), and time per output token (TPOT)
\cite{niu2026tokenpowerbench}.  Selecting an energy-efficient serving
configuration for a target workload is therefore a central deployment
decision.

A direct search would instantiate each candidate on its target accelerator and
measure both energy and service-level objectives (SLOs).  This is expensive on
HPC clusters: H100-class GPU nodes are scarce and queue-delayed, each trial
consumes accelerator time, and multi-GPU or multi-node configurations magnify
the cost.  CPU allocations can instead execute deterministic projections
without reserving accelerators, making them attractive for pre-screening.
Their estimates, however, are not hardware evidence.

Measurement systems establish what a completed run consumed
\cite{samsi2023words,fernandez2025energy,chung2025mlenergy}; \bench{} adds
phase-aligned device and node telemetry across LLM inference configurations
\cite{niu2026tokenpowerbench}.  Serving simulators and learned recommendation
systems reduce live experimentation \cite{agrawal2024vidur,lazuka2024llmpilot,
yang2026charon}.  Pre-deployment energy modeling nevertheless has a separate
trust question: does a cheap model preserve the energy--SLO ordering that drives
the next experiment, and can its failure be detected before deployment?

We ask: \emph{how can CPU-accessible projections and sparse real measurements
support auditable screening of unseen workload profiles before an energy-aware
serving search purchases target-GPU trials?}  \system{} is not a Docker image
or a set of virtual GPUs.  It is a multi-fidelity workflow comprising a
CPU-resident analytical model, a restricted real-GPU executor, and an evidence
gate.  Docker isolates and pins measured execution; the sandbox is the larger
HPC resource and evidence contract shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:sandbox-ladder}.

This work makes three bounded contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A CPU-first, GPU-verified evidence contract that separates workload
  from deployment controls and rejects comparisons with mismatched provenance
  or chronology.
  \item An interpretable workload-transfer model that combines analytical
  compute, memory, and power terms with a four-feature log-residual fitted only
  on development workloads.
  \item Two sealed H100/Qwen2.5-7B holdouts with \BlindMeasurements{} real
  measurements: \CalibratedEnergyMAPE{} and \ConfirmationEnergyMAPE{} energy
  MAPE, plus a preregistered decision that releases TTFT at concurrency four
  and abstains below it.
\end{itemize}

The contribution is the evidence substrate, not yet an LLM agent, a GPU-queue
scheduler, or a verified TP/PP simulator.  Search policies can consume it only
where the measured scope gate permits.
