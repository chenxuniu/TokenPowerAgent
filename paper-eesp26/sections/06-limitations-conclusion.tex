\section{Limitations and Conclusion}

The evidence covers one model, one H100, one fixed serving configuration,
fixed 128-token outputs, and workload transfer only.  It does not establish
configuration ranking, finite-rate queueing, quality equivalence, power-cap
transfer, TP/PP fidelity, H200/B200 transfer, or net deployment energy savings.
Six development workloads are small for a four-feature model, and the
low-concurrency TTFT outlier shows that an axis-aligned scope envelope is too
permissive near sparse corners.  DCGM measures GPU energy but excludes host,
network, cooling, and embodied costs.  The current prediction intervals are
engineering bounds, not calibrated confidence intervals.

Within that boundary, \system{} converts sparse real evidence into useful
screening signals: on eight preregistered unseen workloads it reaches
\CalibratedEnergyMAPE{} energy MAPE and \EnergyPairwisePct{} pairwise
energy-order accuracy, while retaining the measurements and failure modes
behind those numbers.  The central result is therefore not that a Docker
container simulates an H100.  It is that a measured analytical sandbox can
reduce experiments only when prediction, provenance, abstention, and real
validation are one system.  The next step is to test whether the same contract
reduces measured GPU-hours for SLO-constrained configuration and topology
search.
